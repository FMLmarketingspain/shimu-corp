Empowering Women in Mining: How Shimu Helps Widows in Tanzania Support Themselves

When a woman in rural Tanzania loses her husband, she often loses the family's income on the same day. At Shimu Mining Corporation Ltd. we believe the best answer is a job she can rely on. We are a women-led mining cooperative in Tanzania, and most of the people we employ are women, including widows who are now the only earners in their homes.

This article explains why empowering women in mining matters to us, what widowhood means for women here, and how steady, fairly paid work lets a widow support herself and her children.

What widowhood means for women in Tanzania

A widow in Tanzania can lose her income, her home and her farmland at the same time. Tanzania's constitution guarantees equal rights to own property. In many rural areas, though, customary inheritance rules are applied instead, and they often give a widow no right to inherit from her husband (Thomson Reuters Foundation).

The result is that relatives may claim the land and the house, and the widow is left to raise her children with nothing to fall back on. Many widows turn to casual, low-paid work that changes from day to day.

Reform is under way. Tanzania's new National Land Policy aims to remove the customary rules that stop widows and other women from inheriting land (The Guardian, Tanzania). But legal change takes years, and a mother needs to feed her children this week. A dependable wage fills that gap.

Why mining is an opportunity for women

In Tanzania's mining regions, a mine is often one of the few employers that can pay a regular cash wage. Small-scale mining drives much of the sector, and women already make up an estimated 25–30% of that workforce (IPIS, Voices from Tanzania).

The problem is the kind of work women are given. The same research finds that women in mining are often kept in insecure, low-paying roles and see little of the benefit. They face barriers to equipment, training and leadership positions.

So the question is not whether women can work in mining. They already do. The question is whether they are paid fairly, kept safe and given a say. That is the gap Shimu Mining exists to close.

How Shimu Mining employs and supports widows

Shimu Mining Corporation Ltd. is led by women, and most of the people we employ are women. We make a point of offering work to widows, because they are so often the only earners their families have.

Our aim is simple: a widow who works with us should be able to support herself. We do not want her to depend on relatives, on handouts or on us for anything other than a fair wage for a fair day's work.

What a steady income changes for a widow and her family

A regular wage gives a widow choices she did not have before. It does not solve everything, but it changes the things that matter most day to day:

  • Food on the table. She can plan meals for the week instead of finding money one day at a time.
  • Children in school. Uniforms, books and exam costs stop being a reason to keep a child at home.
  • Healthcare when it is needed. A clinic visit no longer means borrowing.
  • Savings and security. Even small savings protect a family when someone falls ill or a harvest fails.
  • A voice of her own. A woman who earns her own money has more say over her home, her children and her future.

The effect reaches beyond one household. When women earn, more money is spent locally on food, schooling and small businesses, and girls grow up seeing women who work, earn and lead.

Safe, fair and dignified work

Empowering women in mining means more than putting women on the payroll. A job only changes a life if it is a good job. For us that means four things:

  • Fair pay. Women are paid properly and on time for the work they do.
  • Safety first. Mining carries real risks, so training and protective equipment come before production.
  • Respect. Every woman has the right to work free from harassment and discrimination.
  • Room to grow. Women learn skills that last and can move into roles with more responsibility.

How you can support women in mining

You can help us offer more widows a job they can build a life on. There are three practical ways to get involved:

  • Buy responsibly. If you source minerals, choose suppliers who employ women fairly and can show it.
  • Partner with us. We welcome organisations, funders and businesses who want to help us train and employ more women.
  • Share the story. Tell others that women in mining in Tanzania are not a problem to solve. They are skilled workers who deserve a fair chance.

Frequently asked questions

Do women work in mines in Tanzania?

Yes. Women make up an estimated 25–30% of the workforce in Tanzania's small-scale mining sector. Many are held back in low-paid, insecure roles, which is why fair employment matters so much.

Why do widows in Tanzania need support?

In many rural areas, customary inheritance rules mean a widow cannot inherit her husband's land or home. She can lose her income and her property at once, while still caring for her children.

How does a job in mining empower women?

A regular wage lets a woman pay for food, school and healthcare without depending on others. It also gives her savings, skills and a stronger voice at home and in her community.

What does Shimu Mining do?

Shimu Mining Corporation Ltd. is a women-led mining cooperative in Tanzania. We employ mainly women and offer work to widows so that they can support themselves and their families.

How can I support women in mining in Tanzania?

Source minerals from suppliers who employ women fairly, partner with organisations that train and employ women, and share their stories.
