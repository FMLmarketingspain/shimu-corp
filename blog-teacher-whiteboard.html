When the Blackboard Becomes a Barrier: Why Teachers with Albinism Suffer Most in Tanzania's Classrooms

Walk into almost any classroom in rural Tanzania and you'll see the same scene that's played out for generations: a teacher standing at a blackboard, chalk in hand, writing out today's lesson while a fine cloud of white dust drifts through the air. For most teachers, it's just an old, slightly annoying habit of the job. But for teachers living with albinism, that same blackboard can turn every single school day into a genuine struggle — one that has very little to do with teaching ability and everything to do with biology.

It's Not "Just" a Vision Problem

Albinism is a genetic condition that means the body produces little to no melanin — the pigment that gives skin, hair and eyes their colour. In Tanzania, albinism is far more common than in most of the world: roughly 1 in every 1,400 to 2,600 people has it, compared to about 1 in 20,000 globally. So this isn't a rare, abstract issue. It affects a real and visible part of the teaching workforce.

What most people don't realise is that melanin doesn't just colour your skin — it's also essential for how your eyes develop and function. Without it, people with albinism almost always have significantly reduced vision, along with light sensitivity and involuntary eye movements that make focusing on anything even harder. This isn't something glasses can simply fix. It's a structural difference in the eye itself.

Now put that person in front of a blackboard.

Most of the documented evidence on this comes from research into students with albinism struggling to see the board clearly — a challenge confirmed by multiple independent studies across Tanzanian classrooms. But the biology behind it doesn't stop at the school desk — the same low vision, light sensitivity and glare sensitivity affect anyone with albinism standing at the front of the class too. If it makes the board hard to read from a seat ten feet away, it makes writing on that same board, up close, for six hours a day, every working day of a career, considerably harder still.

Why Blackboards Are Especially Cruel for Teachers with Albinism

A blackboard relies on contrast — white or yellow chalk against a dark, often glare-prone surface — usually under Tanzania's intense equatorial light pouring through windows or a poorly lit classroom. For a teacher with low vision and light sensitivity, this is close to a worst-case combination:

  • Glare and low contrast. Old, worn blackboards often shine under sunlight, washing out the chalk marks. Students with albinism are widely reported to struggle reading the board from their desks for exactly this reason — it's reasonable to expect a teacher standing right in front of it, checking their own handwriting is straight and legible, would feel the same effect, likely more acutely.
  • Chalk dust in the air. Every stroke of chalk releases fine dust into the air right where the teacher is standing. For eyes already prone to irritation and light sensitivity, that's a source of ongoing discomfort that a student sitting further back simply doesn't experience in the same concentration.
  • Extreme sun sensitivity. Albinism also means skin with virtually no natural protection against UV rays, a well-documented lifelong health risk. Many rural Tanzanian classrooms are positioned to catch maximum daylight, and teachers with albinism spend entire careers standing in that light — far more cumulative exposure than a student passing through the same room for a few years.
  • Physical strain that's easy to overlook. It stands to reason that a teacher with low vision may need to stand closer to the board, angle their head, or move back and forth more often just to check what they've written — a repeated, physical toll that builds up across a teaching career in a way it wouldn't for a student who only sees the board from a fixed seat.

None of this affects a teacher's intelligence, knowledge, or dedication. It's purely physical. And yet it can quietly shape a teacher's confidence, energy levels, and even how students perceive them — through no fault of their own.

A Double Burden, Rarely Talked About

Most of the research and awareness around albinism and education in Tanzania focuses on students — and rightly so, since only half of children with albinism complete primary school, and just 10% reach secondary school. But teachers with albinism carry a version of the same burden with far less support and far less attention. They're expected to be the ones at the front of the room, in the brightest, most exposed spot in the building, using a tool that actively works against their biology — for their entire working life, not just a few school years.

It's a bit like asking someone with a severe hearing impairment to run a classroom using a hand-cranked megaphone with a broken speaker, and then quietly wondering why it's exhausting for them.

Why Whiteboards Change the Equation

This is exactly where TASPO's "Whiteboards for All" project makes such a meaningful difference. Swapping blackboards and chalk for whiteboards and markers isn't just a cosmetic upgrade — for a teacher with albinism, it directly removes several of the biggest daily obstacles:

  • No chalk dust means less constant irritation to already sensitive eyes.
  • Dark ink on a bright white surface creates far stronger, more reliable contrast than chalk ever could — especially valuable for low vision.
  • Smoother, easier writing reduces the need to stand unnecessarily close or strain to check the board.

It won't solve every challenge a teacher with albinism faces — sun exposure, glare from windows, and broader awareness in the community still matter enormously. But removing chalk and blackboards from the equation removes one of the most persistent, physical, daily sources of struggle in their working life. Sometimes the most powerful support isn't a grand programme — it's fixing the tool that's been quietly working against someone for years.

The Bigger Picture

Albinism in Tanzania already comes with serious challenges — from safety concerns to social stigma to limited access to skin and eye care. Adding an outdated classroom tool that actively worsens the condition's core symptoms, day in and day out, is a burden that simply doesn't need to exist anymore.

Whiteboards are a small, practical, affordable change. But for a teacher with albinism standing at the front of a Tanzanian classroom, it can mean the difference between a school day that wears them down and one where they can simply focus on what they do best: teaching.

TASPO's "Whiteboards for All" project is working to replace blackboards and chalk in Tanzanian classrooms — protecting the health of students and teachers alike, and levelling the playing field for those who need it most.
